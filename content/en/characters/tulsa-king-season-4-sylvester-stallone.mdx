Dwight Manfredi spent three seasons building an empire out of borrowed land and other people's grudges. In **Tulsa King Season 4**, Sylvester Stallone finally has to defend it — from a governor who wants him erased, a New York family that wants its cut, and a grief he can't punch his way out of. That's why this batch of episodes matters more than any that came before: the charm offensive is over, and the show is asking whether an aging mobster can go legit without losing the only thing that ever made him dangerous.

The **Tulsa King Season 4 Sylvester Stallone** partnership — actor and character fused so tightly that it's hard to tell where the man ends and the cane begins — is still the reason to watch. But around him, the ensemble has swollen to the point where four or five schemes run in parallel at any given moment. Here's a clear-eyed guide to who's who, what everyone wants, and where the season leaves the Manfredi enterprise.

## The Premise: What Actually Changes in Season 4

Season 3 ended with Dwight torching a rival bourbon distiller (Robert Patrick) and inheriting the operation. Season 4 opens with him trying to do something genuinely new: legitimize parts of the business and settle into a quieter life with Margaret Devereaux (Dana Delany), his girlfriend of three seasons.

That plan collapses almost immediately. The season premiere closes on mysterious circumstances that leave Dwight in deep mourning and unsure whether the government considers his holdings legal. That uncertainty is sharpened by the elevation of Lieutenant Governor Amanda Clark (Gretchen Mol) — a by-the-books reformer who already knows exactly how Dwight operates — to the top office in Oklahoma.

Meanwhile, the New York family he left behind is now run by his old nemesis Quiet Ray (James Russo), and it wants a slice of everything he's built in the Southwest. Add a German AI entrepreneur (Flula Borg) using eminent domain to seize Margaret's horse ranch for a data center, and Dwight is fighting on four fronts at once.

## New Antagonists, and Why They Land Unevenly

Critics who screened the full season have been blunt that the villain bench is thinner than in previous years. Amanda Clark works because she's not a gangster — she's a bureaucrat with subpoena power, which is the one weapon Dwight has never had an answer for. The AI founder is a fun, cartoonish irritant, but he exists mostly to put Margaret's ranch in jeopardy.

The weaker links are the traditional muscle. Robert Davi appears as a veteran enforcer who, in one reviewer's assessment, looks like he can barely stand, let alone take out targets. The result is a season where the scariest characters are the ones holding public office or contracts rather than firearms.

## Why Stallone Still Carries the Show

There's a moment early in the season where Dwight is written with a cane — explained away as a recurring knee injury. It reads as an accommodation for the actor as much as the character, and it changes the physical grammar of the performance. Dwight no longer wins fights by being faster. He wins because people still believe he'll do the thing he's threatening.

That shift is the most interesting thing **Tulsa King Season 4 Sylvester Stallone** material does. The grief storyline forces Stallone to play something quieter than menace, and even when the script meanders, he's watchable. What's changed is the margin for error: in earlier seasons the ensemble could coast on his charisma, and now the supporting cast has to actively carry weight.

The person doing the most carrying is Annabella Sciorra as Joanne, Dwight's sister. She starts the season as a side collaborator and ends it as the moral center of the family — the one character who can tell Dwight the truth and survive it.

## What This Season Sets Up for the Future

A fifth season has already been announced, and the finale leaves several doors deliberately ajar: the Bevilaqua animus is unresolved, the New York question isn't settled, and the political establishment now has a permanent grudge. There's also the widely reported plan for a Samuel L. Jackson spinoff set in New Orleans, which keeps the door open for crossovers without forcing them.

Fan speculation, per community reports, centers on whether Joanne's expanded role signals a genuine power shift or a one-season detour. Nothing in the season confirms either reading, which is probably intentional.

For viewers deciding whether to start, the honest summary is this: **Tulsa King Season 4** is the messiest installment yet and still one of the more enjoyable shows in the Taylor Sheridan stable. If you want tight plotting, wait. If you want Stallone in a long coat delivering threats in a diner, it delivers.
